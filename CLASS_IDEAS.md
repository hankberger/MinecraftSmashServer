# Fighter identity proposals

These are design proposals for a later class pass, not implemented moves. The current prototype adds shared launch influence, timed shielding and a recovery lesson. Each fighter should have a recognizable decision loop, a signature visible action, and a weakness opponents can exploit.

| Fighter | Core fantasy | Decision loop | Opponent's answer |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Steve | Improvising builder | Place a temporary block, shape an approach, then convert a tool hit into TNT or anvil pressure. | Break his construction or enter close range before the setup is ready. |
| Alex | Parkour duelist | Approach from an angle, confirm a hit, then choose a chase, rebound or retreat. | Shield the approach and punish a missed commitment. |
| Zombie | Persistent tandem brawler | Separate the baby from the owner, threaten two timings, then reunite for a strong tandem hit. | Hit the owner before the delayed strike, or disable the baby and fight the weakened owner. |
| Skeleton | Deliberate marksman | Make space, read a jump or landing, commit to one precise shot, then reposition. | Vary approach height and pressure its committed draw or recovery. |
| Villager | Merchant with a defended workshop | Set up a small trading station, earn a limited purchase, then choose a trap, escape tool or golem. | Contest the station and interrupt the purchase before the defense comes together. |

## Steve: build the exchange

Prototype one temporary, destructible block placed directly in front of Steve. Cap active blocks at two, expire them quickly, prevent placement inside fighters or at ledge grab points, and ensure neither blocks nor their destruction refill air resources. Start with a ground-only placement so the first experiment is about shaping approaches rather than infinite offstage bridges.

Keep sword-tip spacing, shovel lift, the anvil and bat-able TNT. Let the block create the signature sequence: interrupt an approach, jump over the cover, drop an anvil or bat TNT around it. A block should be one decision with existing directional special inputs, not a crafting inventory. Tool resource gathering can wait until construction itself proves fun.

## Alex: make movement her offense

Build on the existing dive bounce, Wind Step and confirmed chains. After a connected Dash Cut, allow one deliberate branch: up for a vaulting follow-up, forward for an aggressive chase, or back for a disengaging hop. Misses and shields retain meaningful recovery. None of these branches refresh the existing double jump or recovery.

Her iconic moment is passing over an opponent's shoulder, bouncing from a hit and choosing a new approach angle. Keep basic damage modest; she earns pressure through positioning. Her primary special could become a quick committed dash instead of sharing the other fighters' hold-to-charge rhythm.

## Zombie: make the duo the whole game plan

Keep the baby visible and vulnerable, but give Buddy Toss a readable reunion phase. A second utility press during its return could call one delayed pounce, with an obvious windup and a shared cooldown. Positioning the baby behind an opponent creates a two-sided threat; throwing it carelessly leaves the owner alone.

Give the adult slow, forceful movement and close-range throws or shoves only if shielding becomes too dominant. Avoid passive regeneration and automatic companion damage. The exciting moment should come from timing both bodies, with disabling the baby creating a clear counterattack window.

## Skeleton: reward a shot that was earned

Preserve physical arrow gravity and a visible bow draw. Prototype distinct aim choices at release: forward for a direct finishing shot, up for a falling arc that covers a landing, down in the air for a steep interception. Use the existing directional controls and communicate the chosen trajectory during the draw. Do not add homing.

Keep Scatter Retreat useful for making space, but make missing a fully committed shot an opportunity to rush Skeleton. Melee should buy breathing room rather than match dedicated brawlers. Its identity is choosing when and where to fire, not flooding the screen with arrows.

## Villager: win by making a good trade

Prototype a single temporary workstation and a small stock-local emerald budget. Earn a capped token by successfully defending or contesting the station, not simply waiting in a corner or farming a passive summon. Purchases should be quick choices through existing attacks: a sapling trap, a short escape tool, or a clearly announced golem shove.

Keep only a small number of active objects. The bell can signal the purchase and activate an existing trap, making one recognizable sound connect the kit. Opponents must be able to destroy or contest the station, and a lost stock should clear the economy so an early lead cannot accumulate indefinitely. Never connect combat emeralds to account points or paid cosmetics.

## Suggested order and playtest questions

Start with Steve's temporary block and Zombie's reunion timing: both create unmistakable on-screen identities. Follow with Alex's movement branch, then Skeleton's aimed shots. Villager's economy needs the most tuning and should come last.

For each prototype, watch whether a newcomer can describe the fighter after one match, whether two viable decisions arise from its signature mechanic, and whether opponents learn a reliable answer. Track repeated move use, time spent disengaged, recovery success, match length and rematch choices alongside player comments. Reject changes that mostly create waiting, unreadable object clutter or unavoidable sequences.
